Chapter 01 · Module 1 · Week 1

Introduction to AI Application Development

AI application development is not "training a model" — it is turning a model into a system other people can use. This chapter builds the map for the whole semester: what the end-to-end pipeline looks like, which roles and competencies industry actually hires for, which tools make up your workbench, and what "reproducible" must mean before any number you report can be trusted. It also states plainly where AI assistance is welcome in this course and where it is not.

1.1 What AI Application Development Actually Means

Almost everyone who starts this course has one mental picture: a person sits down, trains a model, and the model is "the product". That picture is wrong in a specific and expensive way. A trained model with no data pipeline, no evaluation protocol, no interface and no monitoring is a research artefact, not an application. It answers nobody's question.

1.1.1 Traditional Software vs AI Applications

The difference is not "AI is smarter". It is that the rules have moved.

Traditional SoftwareAI Application
Where behaviour comes fromRules a person wrotePatterns inferred from data
Nature of the outputDeterministic and predictableProbabilistic; a distribution, not a value
How you verify itAssertion: output equals expectationStatistical metrics: accuracy, F1, latency, cost
Where the difficulty livesLogic complexityData quality, generalisation, engineering
Who owns the failureThe author of the ruleThe author of the pipeline — including the failure to generalise
The one sentence to remember Traditional software says "here are the rules"; an AI application says "here are examples, infer the rules". The moment you choose the second, you also accept responsibility for the cases where the inferred rules are wrong.

Spam filtering illustrates it. Hand-written rules ("contains free", "unknown sender") work until a client called Cash writes about a contract. At that point you stop writing rules and start collecting examples — and you inherit a new problem class: the system now fails in ways nobody wrote down.

1.1.2 The Six-Step Pipeline

Every AI application, from a student project to a production system, walks the same six steps. Skipping one does not save time; it moves the cost to a later and more expensive place.

Define

Who is the user, what problem is being solved, what counts as success and what counts as failure. Failure criteria are written down before any modelling, because after you see results you will rationalise anything.

Data

Acquire, clean, split, label, and check the licence. Data sets the ceiling on what is achievable; no amount of modelling raises it.

Model

Baseline first, then a simple model, then a stronger one. The baseline is not optional — without it, "our model reaches 0.87" is a number with no meaning.

Evaluate

Offline metrics, error analysis, and alignment between the metric and the real cost of mistakes. A metric that nobody in the business cares about is decoration.

Deploy

Serve an interface, measure latency and throughput, handle malformed input without a traceback.

Operate

Monitor, version, detect drift, retrain. The most common production failure is not a bad model — it is a good model fed by data that changed.

Trap of the Week The model is steps 3 and 4 out of six. Students who spend 90% of their effort on step 3 usually deliver something that cannot be demonstrated in week 16, because it has no data story and no way to be run.

1.2 The AI Application Stack

1.2.1 Four Layers and Two Cross-Cutting Concerns

When something is broken, the first useful question is "which layer is this?" The answer decides who fixes it and how long it takes.

Application layer

The interface, the API, the business flow. Decides whether anyone actually uses the system. This is where a technically correct model becomes useless if the output cannot be acted upon.

Model layer

Training, fine-tuning, inference, evaluation. The main arena of this course (W4–W12). It is the layer students over-invest in and the one that is easiest to replace.

Data layer

Collection, storage, cleaning, versioning. Sets the ceiling. A leakage bug here produces a beautiful score that is meaningless (see Chapter 3).

Infrastructure layer

Compute (GPU), containers, scheduling. Decides cost and speed. A model that is 2% better and 40× more expensive is a decision, not a win.

Cross-cutting · MLOps

Experiment tracking, versioning, monitoring, and data tests. Without it, "which model is in production?" has no answer. Covered in Week 14.

Cross-cutting · Responsible AI

Fairness, explainability, safety, and privacy. Not a final checklist bolted on at the end — a set of constraints on every other layer. Covered in Week 15.

1.3 The AI Engineer: Roles, Competency and Workflow

1.3.1 Three Core Roles

Data engineer

Turns raw sources into trainable datasets: cleaning, splitting, leakage detection, Dataset/DataLoader. In this course, Week 3 and Lab 2.

Model engineer

Chooses, builds, trains and evaluates models: architectures, loss functions, training loops, hyper-parameter search. Weeks 4–10.

Application & deployment engineer

Wraps the model into something stable and usable: interfaces, latency budgets, error contracts, monitoring, versioning. Weeks 13–14.

In a large company these are three different people. In this course — and in most small companies — they are you, on three different days of the week.

1.3.3 A Week in the Life

The workflow below repeats indefinitely. Two steps in it are hard rules in this course's rubric.

Align

Confirm the problem, the metric and the failure criteria with whoever owns the outcome. Write them down.

Explore the data

Distributions, missing values, imbalance, and leakage. This step kills more projects than modelling ever does.

Establish a baseline

Rule one. Get a number with the dumbest method that could work — majority class, random, or a rule of thumb. Improvement has no meaning without it.

Iterate, one variable at a time

Rule two. Change one thing, record the comparison, then change the next. Changing three things at once produces a better number and no knowledge.

Ship and monitor

Serve it, document it, watch it. A model whose inputs drift silently is worse than no model, because people have started trusting it.

1.4 Environment and Toolchain

1.4.1 Why Isolation Comes First

Environment problems are the most under-estimated cost in a machine learning course. A single "it will not run" can consume a week of a fifteen-week project. Isolation is the cheapest insurance available: one environment per project, so installing a dependency for one task cannot break another.

# create an isolated environment and activate it
conda create -n ai-app python=3.11 -y
conda activate ai-app

# PyTorch, CPU build (works on any machine)
conda install pytorch torchvision -c pytorch -y

# snapshot the environment so it can be rebuilt elsewhere
conda env export --no-builds > environment.yml
Common failure A ModuleNotFoundError almost always means the wrong interpreter or kernel is running, not that the package is missing. Check which python and the active environment before reinstalling anything. This single habit resolves the majority of first-week support requests.

1.4.3 The Self-Check

Three lines are enough to prove an environment is usable. Save the output — it is the first piece of evidence in Lab 0.

import sys, torch

print("python :", sys.version.split()[0])
print("torch  :", torch.__version__)
print("device :", "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")

# if a GPU is present, record the model and its memory
if torch.cuda.is_available():
    print("gpu    :", torch.cuda.get_device_name(0))
    print("vram   :", round(
        torch.cuda.get_device_properties(0).total_memory / 1e9, 2), "GB")

Three claims are being verified: the version is the one you installed, the device is detected, and the memory is visible. "It imports" is not one of them.

1.5 Reproducibility: Seed, Config, Version

"It runs on my machine" is a statement about a machine. The standard is stronger: same result, another machine, another day, run by someone else.

1.5.1 The Three Pillars

1 · Seed

Fix every random source: Python's random, NumPy, PyTorch, and CUDA. Miss one and two runs of identical code diverge.

2 · Config

Hyper-parameters, data paths, batch size and split ratios live in a file or a single dictionary — not scattered through the code and not in your memory.

3 · Version

Code version, environment version, data version. Miss one of the three and the result cannot be traced back to anything.

What it buys you

A result anyone can re-check — including you in three days, when you have forgotten what you changed.

1.5.2 Fixing Every Random Source

import random, numpy as np, torch

def set_seed(seed: int = 42) -> None:
    random.seed(seed)                    # Python built-in RNG
    np.random.seed(seed)                 # NumPy
    torch.manual_seed(seed)              # PyTorch (CPU)
    torch.cuda.manual_seed_all(seed)     # every GPU

set_seed(42)
a = torch.rand(3)
set_seed(42)
b = torch.rand(3)
print(torch.equal(a, b))   # True — same seed, same numbers
If two seeded runs still differ Something else is random: a seed set after data was created, an unseeded NumPy call, or a DataLoader with shuffling and multiple workers. Rank the candidates by likelihood and check them in order — do not restart from scratch.

1.5.3 What Reproducibility Does Not Promise

A fixed seed guarantees equivalence within one environment. Across different GPU models, library versions or CPU architectures, small numerical differences are normal and unavoidable. The correct response is to state the environment and the device next to the number — not to quietly keep the best run.

1.6 AI Use Boundaries and Process Traces

This course expects you to use AI tools. They are genuinely good at explaining concepts, drafting code, and finding API names. What they cannot do is take responsibility for the result — which is precisely what your name on the submission means.

Drafting documentation or test casesUsing AI to fabricate results, logs or outputs

1.6.1 The Collaboration Trace

Every use of AI that touches your work goes into AI_USE.md, numbered and dated. The essential part is not the prompt; it is the error you found.

## Trace #01 · 2026-09-15

**What I wanted**: hand-write one gradient-descent step with autograd.

**What the AI gave**: a loop doing w -= lr * w.grad.

**What I found wrong**:
- gradients were never zeroed -> they accumulated, and the loss oscillated;
- shapes (3,) and (3,1) were mixed, so broadcasting returned a wrong result.

**How I fixed it**: added w.grad.zero_(); unified reshape(-1, 1).

**Conclusion**: the skeleton was usable, but both bugs had to be
found by me - that is the requirement in this course.
Grading rule A trace that says only "the AI helped me" scores nothing. A trace must name something the AI got wrong and how you detected it, because that is the part only you could have produced. AI collaboration traces carry 10 of the 50 formative marks.

1.7 Pitfalls and a Debugging Checklist

Checklist
  • Read the error before fixing it. A failed import is a path problem until proven otherwise.
  • One environment per project. Export and commit environment.yml the day you create it.
  • Check the device and report it. CPU-only is fine; "I did not check" is not.
  • Fix all four random sources. Python, NumPy, PyTorch, CUDA.
  • Baseline before improvement. A dumb number first, always.
  • Change one variable at a time. Otherwise your comparison is an anecdote.
  • Put the reproducible command in the README. No command, no credit.
  • Record failures, not just successes. They are the evidence that you did the work.

1.8 Summary and Exercises

Five Takeaways
  1. AI application development is an end-to-end pipeline — define, data, model, evaluate, deploy, operate — and the model is only one link in it.
  2. The stack has four layers (application, model, data, infrastructure) and two cross-cutting concerns (MLOps and Responsible AI). When something breaks, name the layer first.
  3. Competency is defined by deliverables. Every row of the job matrix is something you can show, not something you can recite.
  4. Set the environment up once and properly: isolated environment, verified PyTorch, recorded device, versioned repository.
  5. Reproducibility has three pillars — seed, config, version. Miss one and no number you report can be traced or re-checked.

Exercise 1 · Classify the Layer (Required)

  1. Assign each of the following to one of the four stack layers, and say which role owns it: a FastAPI endpoint, a conda environment file, a stratified train/test split, a P95 latency alarm, a Docker image, a data card.
  2. For two of them, write one sentence explaining what symptom a user would notice if that layer was broken.

Exercise 2 · Translate a Job Advertisement, Then Audit an AI Answer (Required)

  1. Find one real advertisement for an AI or machine learning engineering role.
  2. Rewrite every requirement as a deliverable ("can build the data pipeline for X", "has reduced inference latency by Y").
  3. Mark each against the competency matrix in Section 1.3.2. Which row is demanded most often in the advertisement you chose, and which row is missing entirely?
  4. (+5 bonus) Ask an AI tool to list the competencies of an "AI engineer", then check every claim against a dated source — a live advertisement, an official framework, a company engineering blog. Produce a three-column table: claim, source, verdict. Mark at least one claim as outdated or unsupported and explain why you rejected it. This is the auditing skill the course expects, and it is the only acceptable way to use AI on this exercise.

Exercise 3 · The Seed Experiment (Required)

  1. Create the environment, then run the seed demonstration script twice with set_seed(42) and once without any seed.
  2. Paste the three real output lines into your report. Do not retype them.
  3. Remove one random source at a time (random, then NumPy, then PyTorch) and record which removal first breaks the equality of the two seeded runs.
  4. Write two or three sentences explaining why this course requires the seed, using your own measured difference.

Exercise 4 · Build the Record Repository (Required)

  1. Create a public Git repository with the layout shown in Lab 00: README.md, environment.yml, one directory per week, AI_USE.md.
  2. Write the README so that another person can rebuild your environment and run your script without asking you a question.
  3. Clone it into a different directory and follow your own README exactly. Record every point where you had to guess.

Exercise 5 · Problem-Definition Draft (Required)

Write a one-page problem-definition sheet for a project you would actually want to build. It must contain five things:

  1. the target user, named as specifically as you can;
  2. the problem being solved, in one sentence;
  3. the data you can realistically obtain, and where from (a URL or a named source, not "the internet");
  4. the metric you would use to claim success;
  5. the failure criteria — what would make you conclude that this idea does not work.
