1. Learning Objectives

Knowledge

  • Build a text-to-tensor pipeline. Trace a passage through tokenisation, vocabulary, encoding and padding, and name the classic bug that lives at each of the four steps
  • Explain why the vocabulary must be built from the training split only, and what data leakage means when it is built from everything (this week the model sees a mean of 1592 words though the full corpus has 3142)
  • State what an embedding is and what padding_idx=0 freezes; explain why index 0 and index 1 are reserved for <pad> and <unk>
  • Understand RNN/LSTM gating. Explain what vanishing and exploding gradients break in a plain RNN, and how the forget, input and output gates make "what to remember" a learnable quantity
  • Explain padding and masking: why a fixed-length tensor is needed, why 95.58% of this week's test documents fall short of max_len = 96, and why unmasked pooling reads pad vectors
  • Complete an end-to-end text classification. Read a 6-class experiment as a whole: the arms table, the training curves, the confusion matrix and the error analysis

Skills

  • Write tokenise, build_vocab and encode_batch, and keep the per-sample true length lens alive all the way to the pooling line
  • Build the model Embedding(64, padding_idx=0) → LSTM(96, batch_first=True) → Linear(6) and read its parameter count (166,278 at seed 42)
  • Compute a masked mean with (h * mask).sum(1) / mask.sum(1), and pack a batch with pack_padded_sequence for the last real hidden state
  • Compare five pooling arms by mean ± standard deviation over five seeds, and report the difference between the best and worst (0.2620)
  • Read a confusion matrix, name the worst class pair with its count, and quote the raw text of at least 3 misclassified passages
  • Rank the metrics: report accuracy and macro F1 together, and recognise a below-chance model (macro F1 0.0711 against the 1/6 ≈ 0.1667 chance level)
Attitudes · Civic Education User-centred design: the cost of a text-classification error is not symmetric, so the metric has to fit the business. This week measures it rather than asserting it. The last_step arm reports accuracy 0.2407, which reads as "merely weak"; its macro F1 is 0.0711, below the 6-class chance level of 1/6 ≈ 0.1667. Per-class recall runs from 0.1750 (W1) to 0.6643 (W6) — a 3.80x gap — and only macro F1 makes that gap visible. An accuracy-only report would pass a model that has almost stopped recognising its smallest classes.

4.1 Part 1 · From a passage to a tensor: four steps, one classic bug each (p3-p7, KT15)

An LSTM does not read. It reads an integer tensor of shape (batch, seq_len). Eighty percent of this week's bugs live in the four steps that build that tensor, not in the model, and every one of them surfaces at the end as "low accuracy" rather than at the line that was broken.

StepWhat it doesThe classic bugThis week's evidence
① TokeniseSplit the string into a token sequence. English: lowercase, then the regex [a-z][a-z'-]*, so that don't and state-of-the-art stay wholeSplitting on whitespace and hoping. For Chinese this is almost always wrongWhitespace segmentation gives an OOV rate of 0.9973; character level gives 0.0254 — a factor of 39
② VocabularyMap tokens to integer ids. min_freq=2 drops words seen once; the cap is 6000Building the vocabulary from all the data — that is test information leaking into trainingThis week the model sees a mean of 1592 words, counted on the train split only; the full corpus vocabulary is 3142
③ EncodeReplace each token with its id: <pad>=0, <unk>=1, real words from 2 upForgetting padding_idx=0, so the pad embedding receives gradient and driftsnn.Embedding(vocab, 64, padding_idx=0) keeps the pad vector frozen; this choice is what the mask later keys off
④ PadMake every sample in a batch the same length by appending zerosPadding, then reading the padded positions as if they were wordsThe corpus length is median 35, p95 87, max 285 words; 95.58% of test documents never reach max_len = 96

Why not a public dataset The natural choice would be sklearn.datasets.fetch_20newsgroups, but it depends on figshare, and the download channel was measured to be unavailable (ndownloader.figshare.com returned 202/403 and api.figshare.com returned 403). Using the course's own text also buys three things that matter for a class: zero third-party licence risk, zero download, and a fully offline, reproducible run. The price is size — 748 passages is far too few for 6 classes, and that is exactly the root cause of Part 3.
Chinese cannot be split on whitespace — and this is measurable The same Chinese text (750 segments of this course's own Chapter 1-6 material) was segmented two ways. Whitespace splitting yields 375 train segments and a vocabulary of only 372 "words", of which 0.9946 are singletons; on the test set, of 375 tokens, 0.9973 are out of vocabulary — the whole passage collapses into a single <unk>. Character-level splitting yields a vocabulary of 880 characters, a singleton share of 0.2170, and an OOV rate of only 0.0254 on 14,860 test tokens. In practice you reach for a segmenter (jieba and the like) and fall back to character level — but you never just split on spaces.

4.2 Part 2 · Padding and masking: the quiet bug (p8-p12, KT15)

Two models, identical in every line but the last, tell the whole story. The upper block takes out[:, -1, :] after the LSTM; that is position 96, which on a short document is a pad vector, not the sentence. The lower block uses pack_padded_sequence so the recurrence stops where the real tokens end, and takes the last real hidden state. Same parameter count (166,278), same data, same epochs — the only difference is how (B, 96, 96) is squeezed to (B, 96).

Note last_step: the 6-class chance level is 1/6 ≈ 0.1667, and its macro F1 of 0.0711 is below chance, while its accuracy of 0.2407 merely looks "weak". Bucket by length and the damage shows: the harm is worst exactly where the pad share is largest, and disappears as the documents grow. A second, harder line sits on the boundary: with last_step and seed 42, documents of 96 words or more (n = 5) reach 0.4000, while documents below 96 (n = 108) reach 0.2315 — the long ones have no padded positions, so [-1] really is the sentence end. This rules out "the task is just hard". And a max_len sweep kills the other excuse: packed lands at 0.4572 / 0.4454 / 0.4454 for 48 / 96 / 192 (within one standard deviation), while last_step stays at 0.2891 / 0.2389 / 0.2478. max_len is not the lever; the pooling bug survives every length.

Masking is correctness, not an optimisation Both averaging arms run the same "mean over the time axis" and the code is nearly character-identical; the difference is one boolean. unmasked 0.3929 against masked 0.5027 is +11.0 points, and the standard deviation narrows from ±0.0684 to ±0.0449. Look at which bucket gains most — the shortest one — because that is where the pad vectors dominate the average. An error that "looks like it was never written" is the most expensive kind: nothing crashes, the number just quietly halves.

Regularisation does not rescue it — this week's most important honest result Week 6's two most effective tools (Dropout 0.3 + weight decay 1e-3) are added to this week's best arm. Test accuracy goes 0.5027 → 0.4672, down 3.6 percentage points; macro F1 goes 0.4774 → 0.4433, down 3.4 points; and the standard deviation widens from ±0.0449 to ±0.0595. In Week 6 the same two tools worked, because there the data was sufficient and the model was large. Under a capacity bottleneck and under a data bottleneck, the same technique has opposite correct answers. The bottleneck here is the 523 training passages, not the model's capacity — so you diagnose the bottleneck before you reach for a regulariser, not after.

The last piece of evidence is the recall pattern. Sort the six classes by size and recall tracks it closely (Spearman rho = 0.77): W1 0.1750, W2 0.1867, W3 0.4235, W4 0.4778, W5 0.4211, W6 0.6643. There are two small inversions: W2 (n=75) edges above W1 (n=80), and W5 (n=95) sits just below W4 (n=90). W6's recall is 3.80 times W1's. This is direct evidence that the model is learning the prior, not the content — and it is why "add regularisation" cannot help: what is missing is samples, not constraints.

Read the misclassified passages yourself Printing the matrix is step one; step two is pulling the raw text of the failures. At seed 42 there are 14, and reading them makes the diagnosis immediate: they are fine sentences that belong to no single week. Three kinds recur. (1) A short sentence, where the vocabulary burden is heaviest — true W4, predicted W3, 20 tokens: "Hidden layers transform the features first; the output layer decides afterwards. The same data becomes separable in the new space." (2) A generic engineering sentence, true of any week — true W1, predicted W2, 26 tokens: "Hyper-parameters, data paths, batch size and split ratios live in a file or a single dictionary — not scattered through the code and not in your memory." (3) A term reused across weeks — true W6, predicted W4, 22 tokens (17,226 appears in Week 4 as well): "Explain how a training set of 80 samples against 17,226 parameters produces overfitting on purpose, and why that is the point of the lab." None of the 14 is a modelling mistake; they are the task's boundary. The right move is to write that finding into the report, not to keep tuning.

The error-analysis delivery bar has four checkable items, matching the official homework ("explain why the model does worse on some classes"): ① print the matrix and state three totals (565 / 240 / 0.4248) plus the largest diagonal cell (W6, 93); ② report accuracy and macro F1 together and explain the gap; ③ at least 3 raw misclassified passages with model confidence; ④ one testable hypothesis the next step could falsify, then run it and write the outcome back — win or lose.

4.5 Part 5 · The sequence-model lineage and the M8 hook (p23-p26, KT16)

This week's LSTM is the control arm for all of Module 3. Each upgrade in the lineage fixes one specific defect, and next week the class walks the same three steps itself, with this week's 0.5027 as the baseline.

That is the thing to carry out of this week — and the reason the official note asks you to compare the RNN with the later Transformer.

5. Civic Education & AI Empowerment

[Civic Education] User-centred design: metric choice is a duty

  • The cost of an error is not symmetric, so the metric has to fit the business; the slogan becomes checkable when the same model gives opposite conclusions under two metrics
  • Accuracy can pass a broken model: last_step reports 0.2407, which reads as "weak", while its macro F1 of 0.0711 is below the 6-class chance level 0.1667 — the cost lands on the minority-class users, who are almost never recognised
  • Class size decides who is sacrificed: W1 (n = 80) recalls 0.1750 and W6 (n = 140) recalls 0.6643 — a 3.80x gap, rising overall with class size. Only macro F1 makes it visible; weighted F1 or plain accuracy averages the gap away
  • Recall Week 6, where maximising accuracy was right (balanced data, symmetric costs); here it is wrong, because it rewards guessing the majority prior. Before picking a metric, ask: whose mistake costs more this time?

[AI Empowerment] AI generates the pipeline; you locate the bug

  • Step 1: ask the AI to write a complete PyTorch LSTM forward pass for a 6-class text task with variable-length input, and to explain how it handles padding — demand runnable code, not prose, and do not read the reference implementation first
  • Step 2: find the error by running a checklist against the AI's code — no lens argument (so it can only use [-1]); vocabulary built on all data (leakage); missing padding_idx=0; a bare mean instead of a masked mean (the bare mean is this week's 4th place)
  • Step 3: record the before/after difference in a three-column table — the AI's original code / what you changed it to / the accuracy difference. The third column is mandatory: "I found a bug" does not count, a number does
  • Reference magnitudes for the third column: bare mean → masked mean is +11.0 points (0.3929 → 0.5027); [-1] → packed is +18.4 points (0.2407 → 0.4248)
  • Typical AI answers that must be checked: it will often reach for out[:, -1, :] when it ignores lengths, and it may build the vocabulary from the whole dataset — both are exactly the mistakes this week is about
