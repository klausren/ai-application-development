1. Learning Objectives

Knowledge

  • Distinguish the nested hierarchy AI ⊃ ML ⊃ DL ⊃ GenAI and define each layer
  • Explain the three learning paradigms (supervised / unsupervised / reinforcement) with typical tasks
  • Recount the three waves of AI, the two AI winters and their causes, and the current stage
  • Describe the six stages of the AI project lifecycle, the 80% rule, and the toolchain map
  • Restate the course structure, the 16-week calendar with milestones, and grading (30/15/35/20)
  • Identify three categories of AI ethics risks and the academic-integrity red lines

Skills

  • Classify real-world products into ML / DL / GenAI layers on sight
  • Match career roles to the course modules using the career-mapping table
  • Walk through a known AI application using the six lifecycle stages
Attitudes Build the mental frame that "AI is an engineering system, not magic"; understand the software engineer's core value in AI products (the 80% rule); establish the course learning contract (attendance, assignments, integrity policy).

4. Key Activity Design

Activity 1 · Hierarchy interaction (L2)

Draw the four nested circles AI ⊃ ML ⊃ DL ⊃ GenAI on the board and ask students to place real products: ChatGPT = GenAI, face unlock = DL, spam filtering = ML, Siri Q&A = cross-layer. Follow with a rapid paradigm check: "Does spam filtering have a labeled answer?" (yes → supervised); "User segmentation?" (no → unsupervised); "Game AI learning to fight?" (trial-and-error rewards → reinforcement).

Activity 2 · Career self-assessment (L3)

Hand out the four-role career table (ML Engineer / AI App Engineer / Data Scientist / MLOps Engineer); students circle their preferred direction, tally by show of hands, and the instructor maps each direction to the course modules.

Activity 3 · Ethics micro-discussion (end of L3, 5 min)

Pose a real scenario: "If your canteen-queue model keeps underestimating one window's waiting time and the staff there keep getting complaints — is that a model problem or an ethics problem?" Pairs discuss for 2 minutes; the instructor lands it on the three-risk frame: bias (skewed data), privacy (over-collection), misuse (wrong purpose) — and the conclusion that engineers are responsible for checking before delivery.
