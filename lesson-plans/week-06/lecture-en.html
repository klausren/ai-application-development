Model Evaluation, Overfitting and Regularization

⏱ Weekly 180-min integrated session · this plan covers Review + Introduction 10 min and New Content 80 min, 90 min in total (L1–L5) 📚 Course textbook Chapter 6 (evaluation metrics, overfitting diagnosis, regularization) 🎯 Module 2 · Core Deep Learning and Model Training (Week 5–8) — second week of the module 🧩 Lesson Plan ID: LP-W6-A 🖼 Deck: 27 pages (this part covers p1–p21) 🌐 English

1. Learning Objectives

Knowledge

  • Read a Confusion Matrix and derive precision, recall and F1 from its four cells, and state what accuracy hides when the classes are not balanced
  • Choose an evaluation metric from the business cost of the two error types, instead of from the metric that is easiest to compute
  • Explain the job of each part of the training / validation / test split, and why k-fold cross-validation is steadier when the data set is small
  • Describe the bias-variance trade-off and name the three states of a model — underfitting, well-fitted, overfitting — by the shape of its two curves
  • State what Early Stop, Weight Decay, Dropout, batch normalization and Data Augmentation each do during training, and what each one costs
  • Explain why Validation Loss — not validation accuracy — is the reliable signal that a model has started to overfit

Skills

  • Say whether a training log shows overfitting or underfitting, using the numbers rather than an impression
  • Implement early stopping with a patience counter and record the best epoch it stopped at
  • Set up a six-arm regularization comparison in which one variable changes per arm, repeated over five seeds, reported as mean ± standard deviation
  • Quantify generalization with a single number: the rise of validation loss from its minimum to the last epoch
  • Check an AI-suggested diagnosis against the actual training and validation curves, and delete the explanations the curves do not support
Attitudes Reporting an experiment that did not work is a basic requirement of research integrity. This week's measured table contains arms that failed on purpose — and they are kept, printed and discussed. The professional habit is: regularisation is not a ritual that makes a number go up. Measure it, keep the arm that helped, and say honestly which arm made things worse.

4. Key Activity Design

Activity 1 · The 99% Accuracy That Satisfied Nobody (L2, p4)

Open with the extreme case: a classifier that is right 99 times out of 100, and yet not one customer is satisfied, because the only error it ever makes is the one the customer cares about. Ask the class to name a real business in which this would happen. Then derive precision, recall and F1 from the confusion matrix, and re-read the case with the words "false negative" and "false positive" attached to it. The point of the activity is the direction of the reasoning: the cost comes first, the metric comes second.

Activity 2 · Count the Matrix by Hand (L2, p5–p6)

Hand out a small 10-class confusion matrix and have students compute precision, recall and F1 for one class with pen and paper before any code appears. Then run the code on p6 and compare. Students who computed it by hand immediately see why the per-class numbers differ so much from accuracy — and why a single aggregate number cannot describe a multi-class model.

Activity 3 · Which Curve Moved? (L4, p14–p15)

Show the measured train-versus-validation chart of the baseline arm and ask one question: at which epoch did this model start to overfit? The training accuracy curve answers "never" (it reaches 1.0 and stays there from epoch 61 onward), and the validation accuracy curve also answers "never" (it oscillates between 0.833 and 0.841). Only the validation loss curve answers at all: 0.6357 at epoch 81, then a monotone climb to 0.8672. The lesson is that the diagnostic question is "which curve actually moves", not "what does the training loss do".

Activity 4 · The Arm That Made Things Worse (L5, p20)

Reveal the six-arm table without commenting on the last two rows. Then ask the class to read the Data Augmentation row aloud: training accuracy 0.960 ± 0.027 instead of 1.000, validation accuracy 0.814, test accuracy 0.824 instead of 0.878 — and the combined arm down at 0.740. Ask: if augmentation is a standard technique, why does it lose here? The answer the class must reach is that the training set has 80 images of 8×8 pixels, and shifting a digit by ±1 pixel or adding σ=0.15 noise erases the stroke structure, which is equivalent to manufacturing wrong labels. This is underfitting, not a failed regularization.

Activity 5 · Is 0.006 a Result? (L5, p21)

Write two numbers on the board: Dropout 0.884 ± 0.011 and baseline 0.878 ± 0.012 in test accuracy. The gap is 0.006, and the standard deviation is 0.012 — twice the gap. Let the class vote on whether Dropout is better, then compute. The only defensible sentence is "no significant difference was observed for this comparison", which is exactly the sentence students find hardest to write. This activity is also where the AI-diagnosis exercise starts: an AI will confidently explain why Dropout wins, and the curve and the standard deviation say otherwise.

Teaching Tip · AI-Assisted Diagnosis The AI-assisted step of this session runs in the lab part, but it is set up here: ask the AI for the reasons why the baseline model overfits, and then require every proposed reason to be checked against the train and validation curves. Explanations the curves do not support are deleted, not softened. The deliverable is the shortened list plus the curve evidence for each surviving item.

Protocol for every arm: load_digits (8×8 grayscale, 10 classes, 1797 samples) → normalized to 0–1 → 70/15/15 stratified split → training set of 80 samples only → 64-128-64-10 MLP with 17,226 parameters (about 215 parameters per sample) → Adam at 1e-3, batch 32, 400 epochs → 5 seeds (42, 43, 44, 45, 46), reported as mean ± standard deviation. Fully offline; the whole sweep runs on CPU.

How to Read the Table · Three Honest Conclusions 1. Regularization suppresses the rise of validation loss; it does not raise accuracy. All six arms land between 0.716 and 0.858 in validation accuracy, and every test-accuracy difference sits inside one standard deviation (0.010–0.024). 2. Data Augmentation and the combined arm are harmful on 8×8 images because they cause underfitting: augmentation pulls test accuracy down from 0.878 to 0.824 and training accuracy from 1.000 to 0.960, and the combined arm collapses to 0.740. On 64 pixels, a pixel-level shift erases the stroke structure, which is equivalent to manufacturing wrong labels. 3. A single run cannot settle a comparison: the same change moves by up to ±3 percentage points across seeds, and Dropout 0.884 against baseline 0.878 is a gap of 0.006, smaller than the 0.012 standard deviation.
